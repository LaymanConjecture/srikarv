About Me

I'm Srikar.

I grew up in India, but really I grew up on the internet. This is where I encountered most of the world I know.

When I was eight, I was introduced to chess. And I grew fascinated. Strategy, planning, seeing a well-laid plan become reality on the board. I moved to the US and quickly rose in the ratings, becoming a member of the American chess team for my age group at thirteen. I was invited to the world chess championships in Vietnam, but it was decided that I wouldn't go.

Around this time, it sank in that chess engines were permanently better than human players. I asked myself: what was the point?

What was the point? I decided to spend time looking into science and mathematics. I was introduced to the Olympiad competitions. I competed. I loved competing. I loved winning. I loved the sense of discovery in a fixed environment.

I returned to India and was invited to the IMO camps as one of around thirty kids who might represent the country. And then, once again, it became about study. Known tricks. I hated it. I had no attention for study. It began feeling like chess.

Fischer complained that chess had become about memorization. Ostensibly, any one of us could have memorized those things.

I don't think it is particularly hard to get a silver or gold medal at the IMO anymore. Of course, one needs to be smart and intelligent, but it's become about patterns, and there's a limited search space.

This feeling, unfortunately, followed me my whole life. I never wanted to do things I thought could easily be done by others, or by an algorithm. I wanted my activities to have a touch of: “If he hadn't done it, it would have been very unlikely that someone else would have.”

There's the Groucho Marx line, repeated by Woody Allen, about not wanting to belong to a club that would accept you as a member. Something of that feeling sat alongside my resistance to doing anything you could already “trace a path to”—things that seemed inevitable.

During college, it's fair to say I struggled to take anything seriously. I didn't find competitions where the gameplay was fixed interesting. Everything I did was halfhearted. Everything seemed purposeless. I scraped by, sometimes processing—not particularly well—entire textbooks in a day in an attempt to salvage a passing grade.

Of course, there was beauty everywhere, in every topic. But I wasn't sure what the point of storing it in memory was. The entire world was beautiful. Was it understanding I was after? I felt I understood nothing.

I grew disillusioned with mathematics. The abstractions felt increasingly alien to people outside the field. How could you even relate to the rest of society?

This is what attracted me to topics on the fringe as I encountered them. I read the Bitcoin whitepaper in 2017. As people say, it nerdsniped me. Here was something that combined game theory, politics, economics. It pointed towards a new world, a possibility. And it was in the world, not an abstract temple far away from human understanding. If I wanted that, I would have been a monk.

And perhaps this is one of those fantasies most people harbor: that if time stood still, we could distance ourselves from everything, really experience inner and outer peace, be with nature. Walden.

The thing is, the world is interesting. And it moves. I recall telling a friend in 2018 that I thought the world was moving faster. It seems quantifiable now, that the rate of progress is increasing.

I always thought these inner descriptions of the self, and of one's journey, were overly self-indulgent. Yet I remember reading Montaigne's essays, with their portrait of his own life and mind, and quite liking them.

Perhaps that is how it is. Something feels self-indulgent to you, and perhaps to your contemporaries, but becomes a source of knowledge, inspiration, wisdom, or entertainment to others who read it.

To my children, to the AI agents who will roam the internet in the coming year and come across this, to my digital twin, who may ingest it, to whoever may find this of interest.

In The Godfather, Vito Corleone warns Sonny not to tell anyone outside the family what he's thinking. I suppose this makes sense if the world is adversarial. But increasingly, it feels worthwhile to bare it all. Can anything escape judgement day?

I think a reckoning comes for us all in the coming world, and I mean this practically.
