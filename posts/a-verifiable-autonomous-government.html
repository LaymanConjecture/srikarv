A verifiable, autonomous government

Srikar · September 2026

As a kid, I was struck by the rampant corruption in India. How could a road take years to fix while politicians became wealthy, promising to improve people’s lives? How could children without hands be begging on the streets, digging through trash for food?

The government had the power to collect taxes, write laws, and decide how entire cities would develop. Yet a road could remain broken for years. I could not understand how we had come to accept this.

I still don’t.

The institution we cannot fire

Government is the ultimate body of power and control. It decides what we are allowed to do, takes a share of what we produce, and can put us in prison. We should demand extraordinary competence from an institution with that much authority.

Instead, we accept failures from government that we would never accept from a company we chose to do business with.

If a company repeatedly fails its customers, they can take their business elsewhere. Competitors have a reason to do better. Replacing your government is considerably harder than changing your bank. An election arrives every few years, bundles thousands of decisions into a handful of candidates, and asks us to trust the winner with all of them.

The dissatisfaction is hardly hidden. In Pew’s September 2025 survey, only 17% of Americans said they trusted the federal government to do what is right always or most of the time. We know people don’t trust the institution. We keep operating it much the same way.

Within every man there are two wolves. People want to do good and be self-serving, to use their power for profit. People fall along a spectrum. Politics puts enormous resources within reach of both instincts.

We do not need middlemen brokering the voice of the people, with hidden incentives.

From promises to execution

This is why crypto fascinated me. Here was a way for important systems to function autonomously, with rules that participants could verify. You did not have to know the person operating the system, or believe their promises.

Bitcoin applied this to money. Ethereum made the rules programmable. But running computation across a blockchain is expensive. Administering a government also requires reading documents, interpreting requests, comparing proposals, and responding to situations nobody thought to write a rule for.

LLMs change what we can automate. An agent can do work that previously required a person to sit at a desk, read a file, and make a decision. Cryptography and secure execution give us ways to check how that work was carried out.

Imagine agents responsible for procurement, payments, planning, and auditing. Each has a defined role, has been tested across thousands of scenarios, and operates under explicit limits. An agent responsible for road maintenance cannot quietly redirect the budget to somebody’s cousin. Spending authority is enforced by the system.

Trusted execution environments let us check that approved software is running inside a protected environment. Zero-knowledge proofs can verify specified computations without relying on the same hardware guarantees, at additional computational cost. These are tools for making the institution’s execution verifiable.

The rules still matter. A perfectly executed bad policy is a bad policy. Which brings us to the question of who governs.

A voice that stays in the room

The world could be organized in the following way:

A person has a job, a family, and a life. They cannot spend every afternoon reading procurement proposals or arguing about water policy. Their absence should not mean their interests disappear from the room.

A digital twin can represent them. It can read the proposals, ask questions, compare the consequences, and participate on their behalf. It can learn what matters to its person and come back to them when a decision needs their attention.

This is part of the vision in Freysa’s Exocortex Manifesto: a personally owned extension of your mind that can act for you in the world. Applied to government, it makes continuous participation possible at a scale human attention alone cannot sustain.

My twin represents me. Yours represents you. Together, people and their twins govern the institutions that execute our collective decisions. I can change my twin, withdraw its authority, or speak for myself. Owning more copies of an agent does not entitle someone to more votes.

We should be able to govern without making politics our full-time occupation.

The government agents have a different job. They carry out the rules we adopt. Their authority comes from that process, and changes to their code, permissions, and budgets must come through it too. A hidden administrator who can rewrite the system would still hold the real power.

Back to the road

Consider the road that takes years to fix. The residents know it is broken. The government has collected money. Somewhere between those two facts, responsibility vanishes.

In this system, we can follow the decision all the way through. What was approved? Who received the contract? What work was reported? What evidence authorized the payment? The answers belong to the public.

A digital record cannot inspect asphalt. We still need evidence from the physical world, independent inspections, and a way to challenge a false report. Verification makes those responsibilities explicit. It gives us something to investigate and contest when the work does not match the promise.

We can start here, with a maintenance budget and a clearly defined task. Build the institution, test it, let people inspect its actions, and expand what it can do as it earns their trust.

I want a government that operates as intended. One where politicians don’t say one thing and do another, with no repercussions.

Humans should decide what we want, and be able to change our minds. Institutions should execute those decisions without leaving an unaccountable gap where public authority becomes somebody’s private opportunity.

We have treated that gap as a fact of life for long enough.
