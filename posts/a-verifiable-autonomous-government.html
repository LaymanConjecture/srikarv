A verifiable, autonomous government

Srikar · September 2026

As a kid, I was struck by the rampant corruption in India. How could a road take years to fix while politicians became wealthy, promising to improve people’s lives? How could children without hands be begging on the streets, digging through trash for food?

The government had the power to collect taxes, write laws, and decide how entire cities would develop. Yet a road could remain broken for years. I could not understand how we had come to accept this.

I still don’t.

In Transparency International’s 2020 survey, 39% of public-service users surveyed in India reported paying a bribe in the preceding year. Forty-six percent had used personal connections to obtain a service. And 63% of respondents in India believed that reporting corruption would bring retaliation. Think about what that asks of a person: pay, find somebody who knows somebody, or risk the consequences of complaining.

The institution we cannot fire

Government is the ultimate body of power and control. It decides what we are allowed to do, takes a share of what we produce, and can put us in prison. We should demand extraordinary competence from an institution with that much authority.

Instead, we accept failures from government that we would never accept from a company we chose to do business with.

If a company repeatedly fails its customers, they can take their business elsewhere. Competitors have a reason to do better. Replacing your government is considerably harder than changing your bank. An election arrives every few years, bundles thousands of decisions into a handful of candidates, and asks us to trust the winner with all of them.

The dissatisfaction is hardly hidden. In Pew’s September 2025 survey, only 17% of Americans said they trusted the federal government to do what is right always or most of the time. We know people don’t trust the institution. We keep operating it much the same way.

The OECD’s 2025 survey, published in 2026, tells a similar story across 33 member countries. On average, 43% expressed low or no trust in their national government, compared with 40% expressing high or moderately high trust. Only 31% believed people like them had a say in what government does. An institution that governs your life should not feel so far beyond your reach.

What about the promises themselves? A 2017 study of more than 20,000 election pledges across 12 countries found that governing parties fulfilled roughly 60% of their pledges at least partially. In the British governments studied, the figure was 86%. These are testable commitments, not every reassuring sentence uttered during a campaign, and partial fulfillment is included.

Politicians do keep many promises. Elections exert pressure, too: a 2020 study covering 69 elections in 14 countries found that higher pledge fulfillment was associated with smaller electoral losses for governing parties. The question is whether waiting until the next election is enough. I want people to be able to see what is happening to their decisions, challenge it, and change course while it still matters.

Within every man there are two wolves. People want to do good and be self-serving, to use their power for profit. People fall along a spectrum. Politics puts enormous resources within reach of both instincts.

We do not need middlemen brokering the voice of the people, with hidden incentives.

Why now?

People have wanted honest, competent government for centuries. What has changed is our ability to build institutions that do the work and make their execution verifiable. Two developments are coming together: improvements in cryptography, and the arrival of capable AI agents.

Crypto was the first part of this that fascinated me. Bitcoin showed that a system could operate without asking us to trust the person running it. Ethereum made the rules programmable. Advances in zero-knowledge proofs have made it practical to verify increasingly substantial computations, as systems like ZK-rollups demonstrate. Alongside them, trusted execution environments provide hardware-backed evidence that approved software is running. These are different guarantees, but both help us hold software to an agreed process.

What was missing was software capable of doing the work. A government deals with documents, competing requests, incomplete information, and situations that nobody anticipated when writing the rules. Until recently, we needed people to interpret all of that.

LLMs and agents change this. They can read documents, write and run code, use tools, compare proposals, and produce work that previously required a person at a desk. The same system can move between the written justification for a project and the spreadsheet that is supposed to fund it.

I am convinced that we no longer need 99% of the politicians currently in office, or 99% of government workers doing desk jobs. Agents can replace almost all of that work today.

I see agents becoming superhuman at most of the work politicians currently do, especially the analysis behind budget decisions. A budget is where a government’s stated priorities meet its actual choices. Read the proposals. Compare costs. Check what similar projects delivered. Model different allocations. Find the commitments that disappeared between the speech and the spreadsheet. This is exactly the work I would give to agents.

There are already concrete examples. Stellenbosch University’s Policy Innovation Lab used an LLM to analyze South Africa’s 2026 executive budget, classifying spending against the government’s stated priorities. Researchers designed the method and checked the output. That demonstrates a piece of the work; the replacement claim above is my assessment of how far we can take it.

People still decide what matters. How much do we value a shorter commute, a better hospital, cleaner air? Agents should work through the allocations and consequences under those priorities, and come back to us when the choices require a decision. Being better at the analysis does not give a model the authority to choose our values.

The confluence matters. Cryptography alone could enforce the rules we knew how to express, but could not do most administrative work. A capable agent alone gives us another decision-maker whose actions we have to trust. Together, they give us a route to institutions that can perform complex work under authority we can inspect and constrain. That is why I think we can build this now.

Imagine agents responsible for procurement, payments, planning, and auditing. Each has a defined role, has been tested across thousands of scenarios, and operates under explicit limits. Spending authority is enforced outside the model: the agent can release funds only through the authorized process. Changes to that process must be governed too.

A voice that stays in the room

The world could be organized in the following way:

A person has a job, a family, and a life. They cannot spend every afternoon reading procurement proposals or arguing about water policy. Their absence should not mean their interests disappear from the room.

A digital twin can represent them. It can read the proposals, ask questions, compare the consequences, and participate on their behalf. It can learn what matters to its person and come back to them when a decision needs their attention.

This is part of the vision in Freysa’s Exocortex Manifesto: a personally owned extension of your mind that can act for you in the world. Applied to government, it makes continuous participation possible at a scale human attention alone cannot sustain.

My twin represents me. Yours represents you. Together, people and their twins govern the institutions that execute our collective decisions. I can change my twin, withdraw its authority, or speak for myself. Owning more copies of an agent does not entitle someone to more votes.

We should be able to govern without making politics our full-time occupation.

The government agents have a different job. They carry out the rules we adopt. Their authority comes from that process, and changes to their code, permissions, and budgets must come through it too. A hidden administrator who can rewrite the system would still hold the real power.

Back to the road

Consider the road that takes years to fix. The residents know it is broken. The government has collected money. Somewhere between those two facts, responsibility vanishes.

In this system, we can follow the decision all the way through. What was approved? Who received the contract? What work was reported? What evidence authorized the payment? The answers belong to the public.

A digital record cannot inspect asphalt. We still need evidence from the physical world, independent inspections, and a way to challenge a false report. Verification makes those responsibilities explicit. It gives us something to investigate and contest when the work does not match the promise.

We can start here, with a maintenance budget and a clearly defined task. Build the institution, test it, let people inspect its actions, and expand what it can do as it earns their trust.

I want a government that operates as intended. One where politicians don’t say one thing and do another, with no repercussions.

Humans should decide what we want, and be able to change our minds. Institutions should execute those decisions without leaving an unaccountable gap where public authority becomes somebody’s private opportunity.

We have treated that gap as a fact of life for long enough.

Sources & technical context
  • The 99% replacement claim is the author’s assessment, not an established empirical estimate. GDPval evaluates bounded professional tasks; it does not establish autonomous replacement of whole government roles or superhuman public-budget decisions. The South African budget example used human-designed categories, review, and checks, and produced classifications rather than autonomous allocation decisions.
  • Transparency International, Global Corruption Barometer: Asia 2020, printed pages 22 and 31. Bribery and connections figures concern surveyed public-service users; the retaliation figure concerns respondents’ expectations. Historical self-reported experiences and perceptions, not a current corruption rate.
  • OECD Trust Survey 2026 Results, 2025 fieldwork. Figures are unweighted averages of country estimates. These questions differ from Pew’s; the percentages are not directly interchangeable. The same report finds 65% satisfaction with administrative services among recent users.
  • Thomson et al. (2017), The Fulfillment of Parties’ Election Pledges. Published version: 57 election campaigns in 12 countries; 5,439 of 9,133 governing-party pledges fulfilled at least partially. The overall study also includes opposition pledges. An earlier manuscript has different sample counts. This is not a worldwide percentage or a measure of deliberate dishonesty.
  • Matthieß (2020), Retrospective pledge voting. Covers elections from 1977–2015. The association with electoral outcomes does not establish that every broken promise is punished.
  • Pew Research Center: public trust in government, 1958–2025. The survey measures trust, rather than government performance directly.
  • Freysa’s Exocortex Manifesto and sovereign agents architecture. The civic arrangement here is a proposal, not a deployed Freysa government.
  • IETF remote attestation architecture. Attestation depends on hardware and verification assumptions. It does not establish that a policy is wise or its external inputs are true.
  • Ethereum: validity proofs. Proofs establish a specified computation; proving a complete agent workload can be costly. External model calls need their own guarantees.
